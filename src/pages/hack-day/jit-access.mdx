# Track 04 · Just-In-Time Access

Standing access is access somebody holds whether they are using it or not. **Just-In-Time access**,
or JIT, replaces it with access that is approved in advance but not provisioned until the person
activates it, then removed automatically when the activation expires.

In this hack you will build that end to end. You create a privileged group in the demo system, teach
your connector to grant and revoke it, put it under your tenant's JIT policy, activate it, and watch
the membership appear in the demo system and then be taken away again without anybody asking.

Nothing here is simulated. The provisioning call is your code, the activation is a real activation
workflow in your tenant, and the deprovisioning is the platform calling your connector again when
the clock runs out. You can see both calls in your connector's log.

**You will need:** a finished [Track 02](/hack-day/saas-connectivity) connector, a terminal, and a
browser.

:::caution Start with Track 02

This track builds on the connector from
[Track 02 · SaaS Connectivity](/hack-day/saas-connectivity). You need its source deployed in your
tenant, with accounts and entitlements aggregated, before step 1 makes sense.

The reason is not convenience. JIT entitlements must come from a **direct-connect source**, meaning
one whose connector the platform can call on demand with no virtual appliance in between. Your
Track 02 connector is exactly that.

:::

## The one idea to hold on to

JIT splits one familiar thing into two separate things:

| | What it means | What it is called |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The assignment** | You are approved for this access. It is yours to turn on. | An **entitlement connection** of type `JIT` |
| **The activation** | The access is switched on right now, and really on the account in the source | A **JIT activation** on that connection |

Standing access collapses both into one. JIT keeps them apart, and every API here acts on one or the
other. When something does not work, first ask which of the two you are looking at.

## What you are building

```
  create a privileged group  ->  add std:account:update  ->  put it under JIT policy
  in the demo system             to your connector            (step 6)
  (step 1)                       (step 3)
                                                                      |
         request it (step 7) ---> you hold the access, but nothing is provisioned
                                                                      |
         activate it (step 9) ---> your connector adds the group
                                                                      |
         hand it back, or wait (step 10) ---> your connector removes it again
```

The interesting moment is the last line, in the case where you wait. Nobody files a ticket, runs a
job, or clicks anything. The activation expires and the platform calls your connector to take the
access back. That is the point of JIT, and it is what you show a developer advocate at the end.

## Objects you will create

Everyone works in a shared tenant, so **put your own name on everything you create.** You will make
a demo-system group called `<your name>-prod-db-admin`, a demo-system account on your source, an
account correlation rule, and an `std:account:update` handler.

## Everyone shares one identity

You log in as `hack.day`, and so does everybody else on this track. That single identity ends up
holding one account per participant, each on a different source, and the access you activate belongs
to it rather than to you personally.

This works, because an entitlement connection is a record of one identity, one account and one
entitlement together. Yours is distinct from everybody else's by its account and its entitlement,
even though the identity is the same. Three things do get crowded, though, and the steps below handle
each one:

| What is shared | What you will see | How the steps handle it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The identity's JIT connections | Everybody's connections in one list | Step 8 filters the list down to your entitlement |
| The approvals queue | Everybody's requests in **My Work → Approvals** | Step 7 tells you to approve only your own |
| The JIT policy | One governed-entitlement list for the tenant | Step 6 reads it and adds to it |

Your demo system is the one thing that stays private. Each demo API key gets its own data partition,
so the `/members` checks throughout this track only ever show your own work. That makes them the most
trustworthy signal on the page.

### 2. Give yourself an account on the source

The 50 accounts from Track 02 belong to fictional people. JIT access is activated by whoever holds

Either way nothing after step 7 will work. Leave that line exactly as it is.

The response contains a `usr_...` id, which the local test in step 3 needs.

#### Correlate it to the shared identity

An account is a row in the demo system. An identity is the person your tenant governs. **Account
correlation** is the rule connecting the two, and without it your account arrives attached to nobody.

One identity holding many accounts across many sources is ordinary, which is why every participant
correlating to `hack.day` is fine. What is not fine is an account that correlates to nothing.

In your source, go to **Account Management → Account Correlation** and add a rule:

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Identity Attribute | `Email` |
| Account Attribute | `email` |

This matches the `developer-relations@sailpoint.com` on your new account against the Email on the
`hack.day` identity. The 50 accounts from Track 02 belong to fictional people, so they match no
identity and stay uncorrelated, which is what you want.

Save it, then run **Account Management → Account Aggregation → Start Aggregation**.

:::tip

**Check:** open the `hack.day` identity under **Admin → Identities**. Its accounts list includes
your account on your SaaS Connectivity Demo source, under the `userName` you chose.

If it does not, aggregation ran but the match failed. The usual cause is an edited `email` line. Put
`developer-relations@sailpoint.com` back with `PATCH /v1/users/{id}` and aggregate again. Do not
carry on, because every remaining step acts on that link.

:::

### 3. Teach your connector to provision

This step makes JIT possible, and it is the one people skip when they think of JIT as a feature you
switch on.

JIT does not store access. It calls your connector. Activation sends `std:account:update` with an
`Add` operation, and expiry sends the same command with a `Remove`. A connector that cannot do those
two things holds a JIT assignment forever and never grants anything.

Your Track 02 connector only reads. Now make it write.

#### Declare the command

Add `std:account:update` to the `commands` array in `connector-spec.json`:

```json
  "commands": [
    "std:test-connection",
    "std:account:list",
    "std:account:read",
    "std:account:update",
    "std:entitlement:list",
    "std:entitlement:read"
  ],
```

The platform only sends commands the spec lists. Until this line exists, the handler below is
unreachable code.

#### Add the membership calls

The demo API treats group membership as its own resource rather than an attribute you overwrite,
which is how most real sources behave. Adding and removing a member are a `PUT` and a `DELETE` on
the same path, and both are idempotent — adding twice reports `changed: false` instead of failing.

In `src/my-client.ts`, add these three methods inside `MyClient`, below `getAllEntitlements()`:

```typescript
    async addEntitlement(accountId: string, entitlementId: string): Promise<any> {
        const response = await this.httpClient.put(`/groups/${entitlementId}/members/${accountId}`)
        return response.data
    }

    async removeEntitlement(accountId: string, entitlementId: string): Promise<any> {
        const response = await this.httpClient.delete(`/groups/${entitlementId}/members/${accountId}`)
        return response.data
    }

    async updateAccount(id: string, updates: Record<string, any>): Promise<any> {
        const response = await this.httpClient.patch(`/users/${id}`, updates)
        return response.data
    }
```

`updateAccount()` uses `PATCH`, which changes only the fields you send. The demo API also has a
`PUT` that replaces the whole account, and anything you omit from a `PUT` body reverts to empty.

#### Write the handler

Chain `.stdAccountUpdate()` onto the same `createConnector()` call in `src/index.ts`. The handler
walks the `changes` array and applies each operation. Three operations can arrive, and `groups`
needs different treatment from an ordinary attribute:

| Operation | On `groups` | On any other attribute |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Add` | `PUT` each value onto the membership endpoint | Never sent. `Add` only works on multi-valued attributes. |
| `Remove` | `DELETE` each value from the membership endpoint | `PATCH` the attribute to `null` |
| `Set` | `PATCH` `groups` to exactly the values sent, replacing the list | `PATCH` the attribute to the value sent |

One detail catches people out. A single value arrives as a bare string and several arrive as an
array, so normalize before you loop.

Paste the handler and fill in the two `TODO` lines — one call to `myClient.addEntitlement()` and one
to `myClient.removeEntitlement()`, each taking the account id and the single group value:

```typescript
        .stdAccountUpdate(
            async (context: Context, input: StdAccountUpdateInput, res: Response<StdAccountUpdateOutput>) => {
                const id = input.key && 'simple' in input.key ? input.key.simple.id : input.identity
                const attributeUpdates: Record<string, any> = {}

                for (const change of input.changes) {
                    const values = Array.isArray(change.value) ? change.value : [change.value]

                    if (change.attribute === 'groups' && change.op !== AttributeChangeOp.Set) {
                        for (const value of values) {
                            if (change.op === AttributeChangeOp.Add) {
                                // TODO: add the account to this group
                                logger.info(`Granted ${value} to ${id}`)
                            } else {
                                // TODO: remove the account from this group
                                logger.info(`Revoked ${value} from ${id}`)
                            }
                        }
                    } else if (change.op === AttributeChangeOp.Remove) {
                        attributeUpdates[change.attribute] = null
                    } else {
                        attributeUpdates[change.attribute] = change.value
                    }
                }

                if (Object.keys(attributeUpdates).length > 0) {
                    await myClient.updateAccount(id, attributeUpdates)
                }

                // Read the account back so the platform records what the source actually holds now,
                // rather than what we asked it to hold.
                const account = await myClient.getAccount(id)
                res.send({
                    identity: account.id,
                    uuid: account.id,
                    disabled: !account.active,
                    locked: account.locked,
                    attributes: {
                        id: account.id,
                        userName: account.userName,
                        displayName: account.displayName,
                        firstName: account.firstName,
                        lastName: account.lastName,
                        email: account.email,
                        department: account.department,
                        title: account.title,
                        groups: account.groups,
                    },
                })
            }
        )
```

Add `AttributeChangeOp`, `StdAccountUpdateInput`, and `StdAccountUpdateOutput` to your imports from
`@sailpoint/connector-sdk`. `AttributeChangeOp` is the enum holding `Add`, `Remove`, and `Set`, so
you compare against it rather than against strings.

Reading the account back matters more than it looks. The platform uses your response to update its
own record of the account, which is what keeps the tenant and the source from drifting apart. One
exception the handler above does not bother with: an update that changed nothing must return `{}`.

### 6. Put the entitlement under JIT policy

Until now you have built an ordinary requestable entitlement. This step changes what happens when
somebody is granted it.

Read the policy first. **You have to**, because there is one policy for the whole tenant and you are
adding to a list other people are also in:

```bash
sail api get /jit-activation-config/v1/policy \
  -H "X-SailPoint-Experimental:true" --pretty
```

```json
{
  "id": "6d38a587-302a-41d1-a3b1-ffae2c25c960",
  "entitlementIds": ["ae735f40-4de9-4163-801d-4a1444e59d35"],
  "maxActivationPeriodMins": 60,
  "maxActivationPeriodExtensionMins": 30,
  "defaultMaxActivationPeriodMins": 30,
  "defaultMaxActivationPeriodExtensionMins": 30,
  "notificationRecipients": [],
  "notificationTemplate": null,
  "applyToFutureAssignments": true
}
```

Paste that whole response here. The box reads the governed list out of it, adds your entitlement
from step 5, and builds the patch below:

<JitPolicyMerge />

```bash
sail api patch /jit-activation-config/v1/policy \
  -H "X-SailPoint-Experimental:true" \
  --body '[
    {"op": "replace", "path": "/entitlementIds", "value": your-merged-entitlement-ids},
    {"op": "replace", "path": "/applyToFutureAssignments", "value": true}
  ]'
```

:::danger Why the box exists

`replace` overwrites the whole array, so every id already in `entitlementIds` has to appear in the
list you send. Dropping somebody else's id takes their entitlement out of JIT policy without warning
them, and their activation in step 9 then fails for a reason they cannot see from their own terminal.

So this is a read, modify, write on an object the whole room shares, and the box does the modify
step from the list you actually read rather than from an example on a page. If you would rather do it
by hand, that works too: send the ids you read plus yours. Never send a list containing only your
own.

:::

The durations belong to the same shared policy and are in **minutes**. Bringing them down to the
floor shortens the wait in step 10, so set them if nobody has already:

```bash
sail api patch /jit-activation-config/v1/policy \
  -H "X-SailPoint-Experimental:true" \
  --body '[
    {"op": "replace", "path": "/maxActivationPeriodMins", "value": 30},
    {"op": "replace", "path": "/defaultMaxActivationPeriodMins", "value": 30},
    {"op": "replace", "path": "/maxActivationPeriodExtensionMins", "value": 30},
    {"op": "replace", "path": "/defaultMaxActivationPeriodExtensionMins", "value": 30}
  ]'
```

:::caution 30 minutes is the floor

**None of these four values can be below 30.** A smaller number is rejected, and the API description
does not mention the limit, so the error is your first sign of it. Thirty is as short as a JIT window
gets, which is why step 10 gives you a way to see the access removed without waiting it out.

:::

These are tenant-wide too, so agree them with the room once rather than patching over each other.
They are two pairs: `max...` is a ceiling the platform enforces, and `defaultMax...` is what a user
is offered if they express no preference. Each pair covers the first activation and any extension.

:::tip

**Check:** the policy shows your entitlement id in `entitlementIds`, and every id that was there
before is still there.

:::

### 7. Get the assignment, without getting the access

Request the entitlement for yourself in the **Request Center**, then approve it. The owner you set in
step 5 is `hack.day`, so the approval waits under **My Work → Approvals**.

That queue holds everybody's requests, because everybody is `hack.day`. **Approve only the one naming
your entitlement.** Approving somebody else's does not break it, but it does hand them a step they
wanted to do themselves.

Here is the part worth slowing down for. When the request completes, **you do not have the access.**

```bash
curl -s "https://dugfer5z7k.execute-api.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/v1/groups/grp_your_group_id/members" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer sck_your_key_here"
```

Your account is not in the member list, and your connector log shows no `std:account:update`.
Nothing was provisioned, and that is correct.

What you hold instead is an **entitlement connection** of type `JIT`, a record saying you are
approved for this access and may turn it on. Standing access would have ended with the group on your
account. JIT ends with permission to grant yourself the group later.

:::tip

**Check:** the request is complete, and `/members` does **not** contain your account. If it does,
the entitlement was granted as standing access, so go back to step 6 and confirm your entitlement id
really is in `entitlementIds`.

:::

### 9. Activate it, and watch your own code run

One call, and a thirty-minute window opens:

```bash
sail api post /jit-activations/v1/activate \
  -H "X-SailPoint-Experimental:true" \
  --body '{
    "connectionId": "your-connection-id-from-step-8",
    "activationPeriodMins": 30
  }'
```

You get `202 Accepted`, not `200`. The activation runs as a workflow, so the response says it
started rather than that it finished:

```json
{
  "id": "jit-activation-abc123",
  "activationId": "8a9b0c1d-2e3f-4a5b-6c7d-8e9f0a1b2c3d",
  "connectionId": "6c692d9972f8400ca4560a68f62c4c5f",
  "activationPeriodMins": 30,
  "status": "ACTIVATING",
  "startTime": "2026-10-02T14:05:00.000Z"
}
```

The `status` you get back depends on how far the workflow got before it answered, so do not read too
much into the first value. The sequence it moves through describes what is happening to your
connector:

| Status | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| `CREATING` | The activation record is being written |
| `ACTIVATING` | The platform is about to call your connector |
| `PROVISIONED` | Your handler returned successfully. **The access is live.** |
| `DEPROVISIONING` | The window closed and your connector is being called to remove the access |
| `COMPLETED` | The access is gone and the activation is finished |
| `INVALID` / `ERROR` | Validation failed, or your connector threw |
| `REVOKED` | An administrator took the access away before it expired |

Watch your connector being called, in a second terminal:

```bash
sail conn logs tail
```

Within a few seconds you see your `Granted grp_... to usr_...` line from step 3. That is the platform
provisioning JIT access through code you wrote.

Then confirm the access reached the source, and find out when it ends:

```bash
sail api get /entitlement-connections/v1/current-identity \
  -q filters='type eq "JIT" and entitlement.value eq "grp_your_group_id"' \
  --pretty
```

`jit.activation` and `jit.provisioned` are filled in now, and `jit.expiration` tells you when your
access disappears.

:::tip

**Check:** your connector log shows the grant, and `jit.expiration` is about thirty minutes away.
**Note that time.**

:::

### 10. Watch it go away

Thirty minutes is the shortest JIT window the platform allows, so you have a choice. You can hand the
access back now and see the revoke immediately, or you can leave it alone and let the clock do it.
Both run exactly the same code in your connector, so do the first one now and start the second in the
background.

#### Hand it back now

```bash
sail api post /jit-activations/v1/deactivate \
  -H "X-SailPoint-Experimental:true" \
  --body '{"connectionId": "your-connection-id-from-step-8"}'
```

Another `202`. Keep `sail conn logs tail` running, and within a few seconds your log prints
`Revoked grp_... from usr_...`. That is the platform calling your connector to take the access back.

Confirm it in the demo system:

```bash
curl -s "https://dugfer5z7k.execute-api.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/v1/groups/grp_your_group_id/members" \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer sck_your_key_here"
```

Your account is gone from `members`. Your assignment is not gone, though:

```bash
sail api get /entitlement-connections/v1/current-identity \
  -q filters='type eq "JIT" and entitlement.value eq "grp_your_group_id"' \
  --pretty
```

`type` is still `JIT`, `state` is back to `Available`, and `jit.deactivation` and `jit.deprovision`
have times on them. You are still approved for this access. You simply do not hold it. That is the
two-part idea from the top of this page, visible in one JSON object: the assignment outlived the
activation.

Read the history of it with
`sail api get /jit-activation-history/v1/current-identity -H "X-SailPoint-Experimental:true"`. A
`403` there means the activation history feature is not enabled in your tenant, and the rest of the
track is unaffected.

#### Then let the clock do it instead

What you just saw was you asking for the access back. Expiry is the same thing with nobody asking,
and it is the part of JIT that matters. So run step 9 again, note the new `jit.expiration`, and leave
it:

```bash
sail api post /jit-activations/v1/activate \
  -H "X-SailPoint-Experimental:true" \
  --body '{
    "connectionId": "your-connection-id-from-step-8",
    "activationPeriodMins": 30
  }'
```

Now go and do step 11 for half an hour, or go and find a developer advocate. Leave
`sail conn logs tail` running in its terminal while you do.

When the clock passes, nobody will have touched anything. No ticket, no job on your laptop, no click.
Your log prints `Revoked grp_... from usr_...` on its own, and `/members` is empty again.

:::tip

**You are done.** Show a developer advocate three things: the demo system's member list before and
after an activation, your connector's own log lines granting and revoking, and the `jit` timestamps
on the connection. That is a full JIT lifecycle running on a source you built.

The deactivation proves your revoke path works. The expiry, when it lands, proves nobody had to ask.

:::

### 11. Stretch goals

- **Extend a live activation.** Add time with `POST /jit-activations/v1/extend`, which takes the same
  `connectionId` plus `activationPeriodExtensionMins`. The same 30-minute floor applies, so the
  shortest extension you can ask for is another half hour. Watch what reaches your connector: an
  extension moves the expiry, so there is no `std:account:update` at all. The access was already
  provisioned, and nothing about it changed.
- **Email somebody when privileged access turns on.** Build a workflow on the **Provisioning
  Completed** trigger, filter it to your source, and send mail when the payload's
  `attributeRequests` contains an `Add` on `groups`. The same workflow fires on expiry with a
  `Remove`, so one workflow reports both ends of the window.
  [Track 01](/hack-day/jit-workflows) builds the email step. Simpler alternative: put an address in
  the policy's `notificationRecipients`.
- **Report on standing access that should not be standing.** `GET /entitlement-connections/v1` with
  `filters=type eq "STANDING"` lists assignments nobody has to activate. Join it against
  `entitlement.privilegeLevel.effective` to find the privileged ones. That list is the business case
  for JIT, in one API call. To convert them, `POST /entitlement-connections/v1` takes up to 100
  `{connectionId, type}` pairs and switches assignments between `JIT` and `STANDING`.
